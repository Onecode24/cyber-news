01 · Breaches & Incidents

CISA warns of coordinated attacks hijacking water utility PLCs

Since July 27, water and wastewater utilities in at least seven U.S. states have reported attackers hijacking internet-exposed programmable logic controllers (Rockwell/Allen-Bradley, Siemens, Schneider Electric), locking out operators by changing passwords and IP addresses — some incidents triggered boil-water notices. CISA is urging utilities to pull PLCs off the public internet immediately.

02 · Vulnerabilities & Patches

Cisco firewall zero-day actively exploited via static credentials

CVE-2026-20316 lets a remote attacker log into Secure Firewall Management Center using a built-in low-privilege account with a hardcoded password, then chain it with other flaws to pull sensitive data. Cisco confirmed active exploitation; CISA added it to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog and gave federal agencies until Aug 1 to patch. No workaround exists — apply Cisco's hotfix now.

CVE-2026-59309 (authentication bypass) and CVE-2026-59310 (directory traversal) can be chained by a network attacker to break into vCenter Server and execute arbitrary code — a serious risk for any organization running VMware virtualization infrastructure. Patch immediately.

N-able shipped a real fix in build 2026.3.1.7 on Aug 2 — MSPs running N-central should upgrade without delay.

Beginner Pick · Learn the Fundamentals

The XZ Utils backdoor (2024)

Not breaking news — a foundational case study every newcomer to security should know.

In early 2024, a Microsoft engineer named Andres Freund noticed something small and strange: SSH logins on a Debian test system were running about half a second slower than normal. Instead of shrugging it off, he investigated — and discovered that someone had spent over two years quietly earning the trust of the maintainer of xz, a compression library baked into nearly every Linux system on Earth, before secretly slipping a backdoor into its code.

Had it reached stable Linux releases, that backdoor could have let its creator remotely take over millions of servers by hijacking SSH, the protocol administrators use to log in and manage machines from anywhere. It's regarded as one of the most sophisticated supply-chain attacks ever discovered — and it was caught almost entirely by accident, because one engineer refused to ignore a "boring" performance quirk. For a newcomer, it's a great lesson in two things at once: how much of the internet quietly depends on volunteer-run open-source projects, and how the tiniest anomaly can be the only visible thread leading back to something very serious.
