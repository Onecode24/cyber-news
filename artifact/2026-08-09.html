The XZ Utils Backdoor: how one engineer's 500-millisecond hunch stopped a global hack

In late March 2024, a Microsoft engineer named Andres Freund noticed something tiny while benchmarking a database: SSH logins on his test machine were taking about half a second longer than they should. Most people would have shrugged it off. Instead, he dug in — and found that a compression library called xz/liblzma, quietly used by countless Linux systems to support SSH (the protocol used to remotely log into servers), had been deliberately sabotaged.

The backdoor wasn't the work of a lone hacker breaking in from outside. It was planted by someone using the handle "Jia Tan," who had spent roughly two years building trust as a helpful open-source contributor before eventually becoming a co-maintainer of the xz project — even pressuring the exhausted original maintainer to hand over more control. Once trusted, "Jia Tan" slipped malicious code into the software's build process, hidden well enough to survive review. Had it shipped widely into major Linux distributions, it could have let attackers remotely bypass authentication on affected servers worldwide.

It didn't get that far, because Freund's small performance quirk led him to catch it before it spread into stable releases used in production. Security researchers now call it one of the most sophisticated supply-chain attacks ever discovered — and one of the closest calls.

Why it matters for beginners: most software you use is built on open-source code maintained by volunteers, often for free and with little support. This story shows how patiently building trust over months or years can be a hacking technique in itself ("social engineering" at the level of an entire software project), why supply-chain security — trusting the code you didn't write yourself — is one of the hardest problems in the field, and how a single attentive person paying attention to something that "felt a little off" can matter more than any firewall.
