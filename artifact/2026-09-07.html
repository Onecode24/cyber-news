Beginner Pick · Field Guide

Colonial Pipeline: how a billing outage shut down a fuel supply

In May 2021, the ransomware gang DarkSide broke into Colonial Pipeline, the company that operates the largest fuel pipeline in the United States, and locked up its billing systems — not the pipeline's actual controls. Unable to accurately bill customers, and unsure how far the intruders had gotten, Colonial proactively shut the pipeline down for six days.

That single decision, not the hack itself, is what caused the chaos: gas shortages, panic-buying, and price spikes across the East Coast, serious enough that the White House declared a state of emergency. Colonial paid the attackers roughly $4.4 million in Bitcoin, part of which the FBI later clawed back — one of the first ransom recoveries carried out at scale.

Why it matters for newcomers: Colonial Pipeline is the textbook case for two ideas that show up constantly in security work. First, attackers don't need to touch the "important" system directly — an ordinary IT breach (billing servers, email, a VPN password) can still force a shutdown of something critical. Second, it's why network segmentation, keeping business IT and operational technology on separate, isolated networks, became a mainstream priority afterward: if Colonial's billing network had been walled off from pipeline operations, a shutdown might not have been the only safe option.
